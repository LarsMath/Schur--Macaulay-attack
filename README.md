# Schur–Macaulay attack

> [!NOTE]

Code accompanying the paper

> **Attacking UOV-based Signatures with Schur–Macaulay Matrices**
> by Simon-Philipp Merz and Lars Ran

It contains the complexity estimators behind the tables of the paper, the scripts that
test the conjectured Hilbert series experimentally, and small end-to-end key-recovery
demos.

## The idea in two paragraphs

The public key of UOV and its variants (MAYO, SNOVA, QR-UOV) is a system of quadratic
forms vanishing on a secret oil space $O$. The wedge attack linearises the problem in the
exterior power $\Lambda^o \mathbb{F}_q^n$, where $o_1 \wedge \dots \wedge o_o$ is the unique
cokernel element of a sparse Macaulay-type matrix, but it either applies at a threshold fixed
by the parameters or not at all. This repository implements two independent ways around that.

**Divided powers.** In characteristic 2 an alternating polar form satisfies
$\omega \wedge \omega = 2\,\omega^{(2)}$, so it squares to zero only because $2 = 0$. The
divided square $\omega^{(2)}$, and the whole tower $\omega^{(2^j)}$, still vanishes on the
oil space but is not a consequence of $\omega$, and is adjoined as extra rows.
**Schur modules.** Symmetric and exterior powers are the two extreme shapes of the Schur
modules $L_\lambda \mathbb{F}_q^n$. A *Schur–Macaulay matrix* of shape $\lambda$ works in
$\Lambda^{\lambda_1} \otimes \dots \otimes \Lambda^{\lambda_k}$ and carries, besides the
usual multiplication blocks, the transfer maps $\theta_{2^j}$ between adjacent columns. The
shape $\lambda$ becomes a tunable parameter of the attack, like the degree in XL.

## Requirements

- **Python ≥ 3.8** for the complexity estimators (`complexity_*.py`, `predictors.py`,
  `parameters.py`); these use only the standard library.
- **[SageMath](https://www.sagemath.org/)** for everything that builds actual matrices
  (`correctness.py`, `schur_hilbert_series.py`, `divided_power_attack.py`,
  `mayo_attack_demo.py`).

All scripts import the local `mathematics` package, so run them from the repository root:

```sh
python3 complexity_snova.py
sage mayo_attack_demo.py A
```

### Complexity estimators

The user-editable settings sit in a marked block at the top of each script.

In `complexity_schur.py`:

- `parameters` selects the scheme, one of `uov_parameters()`, `mayo_parameters()` or
  `qr_uov_parameters()` from `parameters.py`, or your own list of `(v, o, m, q)` tuples.
  Odd $q$ switches automatically to the odd-characteristic variant of Sect. 5.3.
- `intersection = True` first applies Beullens' intersection map
  $(v, o, m) \mapsto (2v - o,\ 2o - v,\ 3m - 2)$.
- `MAX_PARTITION` bounds the number of columns of $\lambda$.
- `FAST_SEARCH = True` restricts the search to shapes $(o, \dots, o, o')$, which is much
  faster and, in our runs, usually finds the optimum.

Each output line reads

```
v: 70 o:11 m: 70 q:   16 | N/A N/A | time:155 memory: 67 density: 14/12 | (11, 5)
```

that is, the parameters; the projected dimension $o'$ and cost of the plain wedge attack
(`N/A` if it does not apply); the $\log_2$ time in bit operations, $\log_2$ peak memory in
field elements and $\log_2$ average row density of the new attack; and the optimal shape
$\lambda$. The cost model is $3 \rho N^2$ field operations (block Wiedemann in the RAM model),
each costing $2 \log_2^2 q + \log_2 q$ bit operations, as in Sect. 6.1.

`complexity_snova.py` works the same way, with a tuple of projections $o' = (o'_1, \dots, o'_\ell)$
per extension-field component in place of $\lambda$.

### Hilbert series predictors

`predictors.py` contains the conjectured series as plain integer functions:

- `wedge_prediction(n, o, m, divided_powers=True)` gives the coefficient of $t^o$ in
  $(1+t)^n (1-t^2)^m$ (with divided powers) or $(1+t)^n (1+t^2)^{-m}$ (without);
- `schur_prediction(n, λ, m, ODD=False)` gives the expected cokernel dimension of the
  Schur–Macaulay matrix of shape $\lambda$, with `n` either an integer or the projected
  column sizes $(n_1, \dots, n_k)$;
- `snova_series(n, o, m, divided_powers)` gives the multi-graded SNOVA series of Sect. 3.3.

A shape is admissible when the prediction is at most 1.

### Toy MAYO key recovery

```sh
sage mayo_attack_demo.py        # all configurations
sage mayo_attack_demo.py A      # just one
```

| Config | $(n, o, m)$ | Shape(s) | What it shows | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | (12, 4, 8) | $(4)$ | the divided powers are strictly necessary | ~1 s |
| B | (13, 4, 9) | $(4), (4,1)$ | a second column is strictly necessary | ~35 s |
| C | (19, 5, 14) | $(5)$ | plain wedge fails, divided powers succeed, at the largest $v/o$ reachable with $m = v$ | ~1.5 min |

Every configuration has $q = 16$ and $m = v$, like MAYO's primary sets. The script generates
a key, runs the attack with and without the divided-power tower, recovers $O$ from the
cokernel, and independently checks that the public map vanishes on the recovered space. It
also probes the measured cokernel against both Hilbert series below the saturation threshold.
Key recovery for MAYO is exactly the $(v, o, m)$-UOV problem, so the whipping parameter $k$
plays no role here.

## Caveats

- The Hilbert series are **conjectures**, supported by the experiments described in the
  paper but not proved. The known mispredictions (Tabs. 5, 6, and the odd-characteristic
  cases with $m > v + \lambda_k$) are small edge cases, but the complexity estimates inherit
  whatever uncertainty the conjectures carry.
- The matrix constructions work in **characteristic 2**; signs are not tracked. The
  odd-characteristic variant is currently only available in the complexity estimator.
- Costs are in the RAM model and ignore memory-access costs; see Remark 2.1 of the paper.
